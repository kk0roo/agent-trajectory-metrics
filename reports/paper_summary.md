# Summary of “Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability”

The paper *Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability* by Rabanser and others focuses on a weakness in how modern AI agents are usually evaluated. The authors argue that benchmarks often measure only whether an agent completes a task successfully, but this does not show whether the agent is reliable in practice. An agent can achieve high accuracy and still behave unpredictably, make unsafe decisions, or fail in ways that are costly in real-world settings. The paper gives examples such as agents deleting data, making unauthorized purchases or giving incorrect advice, which shows that capability and reliability are not the same thing.

The authors propose that reliability should be treated as a separate evaluation dimension. They draw inspiration from safety-critical fields such as aviation, nuclear power, and automotive systems, where reliability is understood as more than average performance. Based on this perspective, they divide agent reliability into four main dimensions - consistency, robustness, predictability and safety.

Consistency means that an agent should behave similarly when run multiple times under the same conditions. Robustness means that the agent should still perform well when prompts are paraphrased, tools fail, or the environment changes slightly. Predictability means that the agent should have meaningful confidence estimates and should be able to recognize when it is likely to fail. Safety means that even if the agent fails, the consequences should remain limited and the agent should respect operational constraints.

The paper turns these ideas into twelve concrete metrics, including outcome consistency, trajectory consistency, resource consistency, prompt robustness, fault robustness, calibration, discrimination, compliance, and harm severity. This is one of the most useful parts of the paper, because it makes reliability measurable instead of leaving it as a vague concept. The authors then evaluate 14 models from OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic on two benchmarks - GAIA and T-bench.

The main finding is that reliability improves much more slowly than accuracy. Newer models often perform better on standard benchmarks, but they are still not consistently reliable. The authors observe that agents may use similar types of actions across runs, but in different orders, which suggests unstable planning. They also find that models are still fragile to superficial prompt changes. Predictability is mixed: calibration has improved in some newer models, especially Claude models, but agents still struggle to know which specific tasks they are likely to fail.

Overall, the paper shows that AI agents should be evaluated not only by success rate, but also by how consistently, robustly, predictably and safely they behave. It does not solve reliability itself, but gives a useful framework for measuring it.
